Abstract

Abstract—Urban local bodies in India face persistent challenges in delivering citizen-centric municipal services, with solid waste management remaining one of the most visible and operationally demanding functions of a city corporation. This paper presents SMART BBMP, a web-based intelligent municipal service platform designed for the Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike (BBMP) that digitizes and streamlines the end-to-end garbage collection workflow while also consolidating a set of common citizen-facing e-governance services under a single unified portal. The core contribution of this work is a three-actor garbage collection pipeline—citizen, waste-collection driver, and municipal administrator—supported by an image-based waste classification module that automatically assigns a waste category, severity level, estimated volume, and illegal-dumping risk score to a citizen-submitted photograph, thereby reducing the manual triage burden on municipal staff. The system further provides a real-time complaint tracking interface for citizens, a route- and task-oriented dashboard for collection drivers, and an analytics-oriented dashboard for administrators. In addition to the waste management module, the platform offers supplementary digital services including property tax payment, trade license application, birth certificate registration, and building permit application, an AI-assisted chatbot for citizen queries, and a predictive analytics module for forecasting waste-generation trends. This paper documents the complete system architecture, the request–response workflow for each actor, the data flow within the garbage collection subsystem, and the design of the supporting e-governance modules. The proposed system demonstrates how a modular, role-based web architecture can substantially reduce the turnaround time of a civic garbage complaint, improve transparency for the citizen, and provide municipal authorities with structured, verifiable data for operational decision-making.

Keywords— Smart City, E-Governance, Solid Waste Management, Municipal Corporation, BBMP, Garbage Collection Workflow, Citizen Complaint Tracking, Image-Based Waste Classification, Role-Based Web Application, Digital Civic Services.

1. Introduction

Rapid urbanization in Indian metropolitan cities has placed an enormous strain on municipal corporations that are responsible for delivering essential civic services such as solid waste collection, property tax administration, trade licensing, and issuance of vital records. Bengaluru, administered by the Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike (BBMP), generates several thousand metric tonnes of municipal solid waste every day. The conventional model of waste management in the city relies heavily on manual complaint registration through phone calls, ward offices, or informal channels, followed by manual dispatch of sanitation workers and vehicles. This model suffers from several structural weaknesses: lack of a verifiable complaint trail, absence of real-time status visibility for the citizen, inconsistent prioritization of complaints (a small litter complaint may be treated identically to a large illegal dumping site), and no structured feedback loop for administrators to plan resource allocation.

Parallel to the waste management problem, citizens are also required to interact with the municipal corporation for a range of unrelated administrative services—paying property tax, applying for a trade license, registering a birth, or seeking approval for a building construction plan. Each of these services has traditionally required a physical visit to a ward office, paper-based documentation, and multi-day processing timelines. The absence of a single digital front-end that unifies these services under one citizen identity increases the cognitive and procedural burden on the public and duplicates effort across departments.

The work presented in this paper, SMART BBMP, addresses these problems through a unified, role-based web platform. The platform is deliberately designed around three distinct actors that participate in the garbage collection workflow—the citizen who raises a complaint, the driver who is responsible for physically collecting the waste, and the administrator who oversees the process and consumes aggregated analytics—because this is the single highest-frequency, highest-impact civic interaction that the system targets. The remaining civic services (property tax, trade license, birth certificate, and building permit) are implemented as lighter-weight, form-driven modules layered onto the same authentication and portal infrastructure, since their workflows are comparatively simple, single-actor (citizen-to-administrator) request–approval processes.

A distinguishing feature of the garbage collection module is the integration of an automated image analysis step at the point of complaint submission. When a citizen photographs a waste pile or an overflowing bin, the system analyses the uploaded image to infer the probable waste category (e.g. plastic, organic, construction debris), assign a severity rating, estimate the physical volume of waste, and flag a probable case of illegal dumping. This automated triage reduces the manual effort that would otherwise be required by a call-centre operator or ward officer to interpret and categorize an incoming complaint, and allows higher-severity complaints to be surfaced earlier to drivers and administrators.

2. Literature Survey

Several strands of prior work inform the design of the proposed system: (i) e-governance service delivery platforms, (ii) smart-city solid waste management systems, and (iii) image-based waste classification research.

2.1 E-Governance Service Delivery

Government-to-citizen (G2C) digital service platforms, such as state-level e-Seva centres and national portals like India’s UMANG and the Karnataka Sakala initiative, have demonstrated that digitizing routine administrative transactions reduces citizen turnaround time and improves accountability through auditable digital trails. However, much of the existing literature and deployed systems treat each civic service (tax, licensing, certificates) as an isolated silo with its own login and workflow, requiring citizens to navigate multiple disconnected portals. Studies on unified citizen portals argue that a single authenticated identity spanning multiple services improves adoption and reduces duplicate data entry, which motivates the unified-portal design adopted in this work.

2.2 Smart Waste Management Systems

Existing smart-waste research is broadly divided into two categories: sensor-based systems that use ultrasonic or weight sensors fitted to physical bins to detect fill-level and trigger collection, and crowdsourced/citizen-reporting systems that rely on the public to report waste incidents via a mobile or web application. Sensor-based systems provide high measurement accuracy but require significant hardware investment and maintenance, which limits their feasibility for large, heterogeneous cities such as Bengaluru where illegal dumping frequently occurs at unmonitored locations rather than at fixed, instrumented bins. Citizen-reporting systems, by contrast, achieve wide spatial coverage at low infrastructure cost because every citizen with a smartphone becomes a potential sensor, but they introduce the challenge of triaging a large, unstructured volume of complaints without overburdening municipal staff. The system proposed in this paper follows the citizen-reporting paradigm and specifically targets the triage problem through automated image analysis.

2.3 Image-Based Waste Classification

Image classification of waste has been studied using convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures trained on labeled waste-image datasets (e.g. the widely cited TrashNet dataset), typically achieving category classification into a small number of classes such as glass, paper, cardboard, plastic, metal, and general trash. Such approaches require a substantial labeled training corpus specific to the target deployment’s waste stream to generalize well. In the absence of a large, BBMP-specific labeled photograph dataset, the present implementation adopts a lightweight, deterministic image-analysis pipeline that extracts colour and luminance features from the uploaded photograph and applies a rule-based classifier to infer the waste category, severity, and estimated volume. This design choice is discussed further in Section 6.3, along with the identified upgrade path toward a fully trained CNN-based classifier once a sufficient labeled dataset of BBMP waste photographs becomes available.

3. Problem Statement and Objectives

3.1 Problem Statement

Citizens of Bengaluru currently lack a single, transparent, and trackable digital channel to report garbage-related civic issues and to access common municipal services. Complaints raised through informal channels (phone calls, in-person visits) are not accompanied by a persistent digital record, cannot be tracked by the citizen after submission, and are not automatically triaged by severity or category before being assigned to field staff. Simultaneously, collection drivers have no structured digital task list that specifies what to collect, where, and in what priority order, and administrators lack consolidated, structured data to identify recurring problem wards or plan resource allocation. There is, therefore, a need for an integrated system that (a) allows citizens to submit a garbage complaint with photographic evidence and receive a trackable reference number, (b) automatically classifies the submitted complaint to assist prioritization, (c) provides collection drivers with an organized, status-driven task interface, and (d) provides administrators with a monitoring dashboard, while also (e) consolidating other frequently used municipal services under the same platform.

3.2 Objectives

  1. To design and implement a citizen-facing web interface for registering a garbage collection complaint with photographic evidence, location/ward details, and an optional anonymous submission mode.
  2. To implement an automated image-analysis pipeline that classifies the submitted waste photograph into a defined category, assigns a severity level, estimates the waste volume, and flags a probable illegal dumping instance.
  3. To design a unique, trackable reference-number scheme so that a citizen can query the live status of a submitted complaint at any time without needing to contact a ward office.
  4. To provide collection drivers with a dedicated dashboard listing assigned collection tasks, ordered by priority and location, along with the ability to update the status of a task as it progresses.
  5. To provide municipal administrators with a monitoring dashboard summarizing complaint volumes, categories, ward-wise distribution, and driver performance.
  6. To extend the same authenticated portal to cover four supplementary e-governance services—property tax payment, trade license application, birth certificate application, and building permit application—so that citizens are not required to use separate systems for routine civic transactions.
  7. To provide supporting citizen-assistance features, namely a rule-based conversational chatbot and a predictive analytics module for forecasting waste-generation trends by ward.

4.1 Presentation Layer

The presentation layer exposes a distinct landing experience to each of the three principal actors upon authentication. A citizen is directed to a citizen dashboard from which the garbage-reporting form, the complaint-tracking interface, and the supplementary e-governance service forms are reachable. A driver is directed to a driver dashboard listing assigned collection tasks. An administrator is directed to an administrative dashboard summarizing city-wide and ward-wise statistics. Role separation is enforced at the point of login, ensuring that a citizen account cannot view driver task lists and a driver account cannot access administrative analytics.

4.2 Application / Service Layer

The service layer is decomposed into independent modules corresponding to each civic function: the garbage collection module (complaint intake, tracking, driver task management), the tax and licensing module (property tax, trade license, birth certificate, building permit), and the assistance module (chatbot and predictive analytics). This modular decomposition allows each service to evolve, be tested, and be maintained independently while sharing a common authentication and navigation shell.

4.3 Image Analysis Subsystem

The image analysis subsystem is invoked exclusively by the garbage collection module whenever a citizen attaches a photograph to a complaint. It performs feature extraction on the image and returns a structured analysis object containing the inferred waste category and its confidence score, an assessed severity level, an estimated waste volume, and an illegal-dumping risk indicator. This structured object is persisted alongside the complaint record and consumed by both the driver dashboard (for prioritisation) and the administrator dashboard (for aggregate reporting).

4.4 Data Store

The data store maintains four broad categories of records: user accounts (citizen, driver, administrator, each associated with a role), garbage complaint records (including the associated image analysis result and the status history), e-governance application records (one table per service type), and ward/zone reference data used for geographic categorisation of complaints and applications.

6.3 AI-Based Waste Image Recognition Process

The image analysis subsystem is invoked automatically as soon as a photograph is attached to a garbage complaint, and its output is used to pre-fill several fields of the citizen’s form as described in Section 6.2. The subsystem performs four independent inference tasks on the submitted image, summarized below and in Figure 6.3.

  1. Colour and luminance feature extraction—the uploaded photograph is rendered onto an in-memory canvas and its pixel data is sampled to compute the average red, green, and blue channel intensities, overall brightness, and colour variance across the image.
  2. Waste category inference—the extracted colour profile is compared against a set of reference rules for each of the nine supported waste categories (plastic, organic, paper, glass, metal, electronic, medical, construction, and mixed) to determine the most probable category and an associated confidence score.
  3. Severity assessment—the apparent image area occupied by the waste, together with the brightness and colour-variance features, is used to assign a severity level of low, medium, high, or critical, which in turn determines a collection priority.
  4. Volume estimation and illegal-dumping detection—the apparent spatial extent of the waste in the photograph is mapped to an estimated volume category (e.g. a few bags, moderate pile, large pile), and a supplementary heuristic evaluates whether the pattern of the image is consistent with an illegal dumping site rather than routine uncollected waste.

It is important to note, for academic completeness, that the present implementation of the image analysis subsystem is a deterministic, rule-based engine operating on colour and luminance features rather than a trained convolutional neural network. This design choice was made deliberately in the absence of a sufficiently large, labeled corpus of BBMP-specific waste photographs, which would be required to train and validate a supervised deep-learning classifier with acceptable accuracy. Section 15 discusses the intended upgrade path toward a CNN-based classifier, including the use of transfer learning from a pretrained image-classification backbone, once such a labeled dataset is available.

6.5 Driver Assignment and Collection Procedure

The driver is the actor responsible for the physical collection of waste once a report has been reviewed and assigned by the administrator. Upon logging into the platform with driver-role credentials, the driver is presented with a driver dashboard listing all reports currently assigned to them, ordered by severity and, where available, geographic proximity. The complete driver procedure is described in the steps below and illustrated in Figure 6.4.

  1. The driver logs in using their driver-specific credentials, which restricts the view to only the driver dashboard and associated task list.
  2. The dashboard displays the list of reports assigned to the driver, each showing the reference ID, ward/location, waste category, severity level, and estimated volume so the driver can plan the collection route and anticipate the vehicle/equipment required.
  3. The driver selects a task and updates its status to IN_PROGRESS when departing to the location, providing a real-time signal to the citizen tracking interface and the administrator dashboard that the complaint is being actioned.
  4. On arrival, the driver physically collects the reported waste. Where the on-site situation differs materially from the submitted photograph (for example, a larger pile than anticipated), the driver may update the estimated volume field to reflect the actual quantity collected.
  5. Once collection is complete, the driver marks the report as COLLECTED, which timestamps the completion and removes the task from the active task list while retaining it in the driver’s completed-tasks history.
  6. The driver dashboard aggregates the number of tasks completed, tasks pending, and tasks in progress for the driver’s current shift, providing a simple productivity overview.

6.6 Citizen Tracking Procedure

Once a citizen has submitted a garbage report and received a reference ID, the citizen may query the live status of the complaint at any time through the “Track Collection” interface, without requiring authentication or a phone call to a ward office. This tracking capability closes the feedback loop between the citizen and the municipal corporation, directly addressing the transparency deficiency identified in the problem statement (Section 3.1), where citizens previously had no means of independently verifying the progress of a complaint once it had been lodged.

6.7 Administrator Monitoring and Verification

The administrator dashboard aggregates all garbage reports across the city and presents them through a set of summary views intended to support day-to-day operational decisions as well as longer-term planning. The principal administrator functions are as follows.

  • Review queue: newly submitted reports are surfaced in descending order of severity, allowing the administrator to prioritise assignment of high-severity and suspected illegal-dumping cases.
  • Assignment: the administrator assigns a reviewed report to a specific driver, taking into account the driver’s current workload and ward familiarity.
  • City-wide and ward-wise summary: the dashboard presents aggregate counts of reports by status, category, and ward, enabling identification of wards with recurring or high-volume complaints.
  • Verification: once a driver marks a report as collected, the administrator reviews the collection confirmation (and any driver-entered notes) before transitioning the report to the final VERIFIED and RESOLVED states.
  • Driver performance overview: the dashboard summarizes the number of tasks completed by each driver over a given period, supporting workload balancing across the collection fleet.

8. AI Chatbot Assistance Module

The platform includes a rule-based conversational assistant that is available to citizens at all times from a persistent chat interface. The chatbot performs keyword and intent matching on the citizen’s typed query and responds with pre-authored guidance covering the most frequent citizen questions, including how to report garbage, how to track an existing complaint, service fee structures, and collection schedules. Quick-action buttons are also presented so that a citizen can navigate directly to the relevant form (for example, “Report Garbage” or “Track My Report”) without needing to type a full query. The chatbot module reduces the load on human helpdesk staff for routine, repetitive queries and is available continuously, independent of ward-office working hours.

9. Predictive Analytics Module

The predictive analytics module operates on historical, ward-level garbage-report records to identify recurring temporal and spatial patterns. It analyses trends across day-of-week and monthly cycles, ranks wards by report volume to identify persistent hotspots, and produces a short-horizon (seven-day) forecast of expected report volumes together with a recommended number of collection teams and vehicles for the forthcoming period. This module is intended to support administrative planning—for example, anticipating an increase in reports around a festival period in a specific ward and pre-allocating additional collection resources rather than reacting only after complaint volumes rise. The forecasting logic in the current implementation operates over the structured historical data accumulated by the garbage collection module described in Section 6, and its accuracy is expected to improve as the volume of genuine historical report data grows following deployment.

13. Results and Discussion

The implemented system successfully demonstrates a complete, trackable garbage complaint lifecycle from citizen submission through driver collection to administrator verification, replacing the previously untracked, phone-call-based complaint process. The introduction of the automated image analysis step at the point of submission removes the need for a citizen to manually classify the waste type or estimate its severity, and provides the administrator with a consistent, structured basis for prioritising the review queue rather than relying solely on the order in which complaints were received. The unique reference-ID tracking mechanism gives citizens independent visibility into complaint progress for the first time, directly addressing the transparency deficiency identified in Section 3.1.

The driver dashboard’s severity-ordered task list allows collection staff to prioritise high-severity and suspected illegal-dumping locations ahead of routine, low-severity complaints, which is expected to improve the responsiveness of the collection process for the most visible and environmentally significant cases. The administrator dashboard’s ward-wise aggregation provides, for the first time in this workflow, a structured dataset from which recurring problem areas can be identified rather than relying on anecdotal knowledge of individual ward officers.

Consolidating four supplementary e-governance services (property tax, trade license, birth certificate, and building permit) under the same authenticated portal as the garbage collection module reduces the number of distinct systems a citizen must learn to use, and the shared workflow pattern illustrated in Figure 7.1 demonstrates that a single generalized application-and-review pipeline can accommodate multiple, otherwise unrelated, municipal services with only field-level customisation.

14. Advantages and Limitations

14.1 Advantages

  • Provides a persistent, trackable digital record for every garbage complaint, in place of untracked phone-based reporting.
  • Automated image-based triage reduces the manual classification burden on both citizens and municipal staff.
  • Severity-based prioritisation ensures that high-impact complaints, including suspected illegal dumping, are surfaced ahead of routine complaints.
  • A single authenticated portal consolidates five distinct municipal services (garbage collection plus four e-governance services), reducing citizen effort.
  • Role-based dashboards ensure that each actor (citizen, driver, administrator) is presented with only the information relevant to their function.
  • The rule-based chatbot and multi-language support improve accessibility for a linguistically diverse citizen population.

14.2 Limitations

  • The current image analysis engine relies on colour and luminance heuristics rather than a trained deep learning model, and its category-classification accuracy is therefore lower than what a properly trained convolutional neural network could achieve given a sufficiently large labeled dataset.
  • The predictive analytics module’s forecasting accuracy is dependent on the volume of genuine historical data accumulated after deployment, and forecasts generated during the initial rollout period may be less reliable.
  • The system does not yet integrate with an external online payment gateway for property tax and other fee-based services; the present implementation computes the applicable fee but does not process the payment transaction itself.
  • Geographic/location capture currently relies on citizen-entered ward information rather than automatic device-based geolocation, which may be less precise for citizens unfamiliar with their exact ward boundary.

15. Future Enhancements

  1. Deep-learning-based waste classification: replace the current rule-based image analysis engine with a convolutional neural network trained via transfer learning on a labeled corpus of BBMP-specific waste photographs, once such a dataset has been collected, to improve classification accuracy while retaining the existing four-output contract (category, severity, volume, dumping risk) so that no downstream module requires modification.
  2. Automatic geolocation capture: use device GPS to automatically tag the precise location of a garbage report, improving on manually entered ward information and enabling map-based visualisation of complaints for administrators.
  3. Online payment gateway integration: extend the property tax and other fee-based services to process payments directly within the platform rather than only computing the fee.
  4. Push notifications: notify citizens automatically via SMS or app notification at each status transition of their garbage report, rather than requiring the citizen to actively check the tracking page.
  5. Route optimisation for drivers: incorporate an optimisation algorithm that sequences a driver’s assigned tasks by travel distance in addition to severity, reducing overall collection travel time.
  6. Integration with a real vehicle-tracking system: combine the driver task dashboard with live GPS-based vehicle tracking so that citizens can view the approaching collection vehicle in real time.

16. Conclusion

This paper presented SMART BBMP, an integrated municipal service platform centred on a fully digitized, trackable garbage collection workflow spanning three actors—citizen, driver, and administrator—and supported by an automated image-based waste classification subsystem. The detailed procedures, flowcharts, and data-flow diagrams presented in Section 6 demonstrate that the citizen reporting, driver collection, and administrator verification processes can be coherently unified around a single garbage-report artefact that carries structured status and classification metadata through its entire lifecycle. The platform additionally consolidates four supplementary e-governance services, a rule-based citizen chatbot, and a predictive analytics module under the same authenticated portal, illustrating that a modular, role-based web architecture can accommodate both the high-frequency, multi-actor garbage collection workflow and simpler, single-cycle administrative services within one coherent system. While the present image analysis engine is heuristic rather than a trained deep-learning model, the structured output contract it produces establishes a clear and low-risk upgrade path toward a fully trained classifier as labeled data becomes available. Taken together, the system demonstrates a practical, deployable approach to improving the transparency, accountability, and operational efficiency of municipal solid waste management, while establishing a foundation for the broader digitisation of urban civic services.
